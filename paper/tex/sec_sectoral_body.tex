Agriculture has the highest calibrated formalization wedge. Its informality rate is $71.8\%$, nearly twice the national average of $44.2\%$ under the broad IBGE definition. Industry ($44.4\%$) and services ($41.2\%$) are closer to the mean. The calibrated wedges range from $\tau_s=1.48$ in services to $\tau_s=5.63$ in agriculture. Payroll-side rural obligations include employer social-security contributions, risk-adjusted accident contributions, and third-party contributions such as SENAR, with reporting rules documented in official payroll guidance \citep{BrasilLei8212,ReceitaESocialRural2021}. The comparison is qualitative, since $\tau_s$ is calibrated to match sectoral informality rather than imposed from statutory rates.

The sectoral exercise applies the national framework separately to agriculture, industry, and services. The aggregate $A_{\text{req}}$ imposes a uniform TFP multiplier on all three sectors and solves by bisection for the value that restores total output. Under the full reform, agriculture requires $A_{\text{req}}=7.60\%$, industry $8.05\%$, and services $6.91\%$, with an aggregate of $7.21\%$. The aggregate is slightly below the national model's conservative headline of $8.18\%$ because this exercise uses PNAD habitual hours rather than DIEESE contracted hours. Industry's $A_{\text{req}}$ edges out agriculture's. At $\sigma>1$ and $\eta_I=0.40$, the reallocation friction scales with the formal base, which is larger in industry than in agriculture despite agriculture's higher baseline informality. All three sectors see informality rise in response to the reform, consistent with mildly substitutable formal and informal labor.

Services accounts for $71.0\%$ of model-implied baseline output and contributes $4.8$ of the $7.2$ pp aggregate output loss. A sector-specific policy exempting agriculture from the cap would reduce aggregate $A_{\text{req}}$ by less than $0.2$ pp. The decomposition treats each sector as an independent instance of the national model and abstracts from cross-sector reallocation of labor and capital. It also collapses firm-size heterogeneity into a single representative firm per sector.
