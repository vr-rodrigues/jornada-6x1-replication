\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Brazilian workweek debate now spans more than one statutory endpoint. PEC 8/2025, a proposed constitutional amendment, was introduced as a four-day, 36-hour proposal \citep{CamaraPEC82025Protocol}. By April 2026, the C\^amara's constitutional committee had approved the admissibility of PEC 8/2025 and PEC 221/2019, a special commission had been created, and C\^amara coverage reported a relator proposal with 40-hour, 5x2, and phased alternatives \citep{CamaraFortyHours2025,CamaraAdmissibility2026,CamaraSpecialCommission2026}. Much of the debate presumes that shorter hours can be absorbed by productivity gains. The quantitative question is how large that gain would have to be at each point in the hours-cap schedule.

This paper computes the one-off TFP gain that would leave aggregate output unchanged in the short run when the statutory workweek is reduced from 44 hours to alternative caps between 44 and 36 hours. I call this gain the output-neutrality benchmark and denote it $A_{\text{req}}$. The object is a scale benchmark, not a forecast of productivity growth and not a welfare criterion. The exercise asks how large a productivity response would have to be to neutralize the mechanical output loss implied by a shorter cap on normal formal hours. Overtime, compliance, wage bargaining, and changes in total employment are left outside the central counterfactual.

The Brazilian setting complicates the calculation. Informality is $37.8\%$ under the narrow PNAD definition and $44.2\%$ under the broad IBGE one, ranging from $41\%$ in services to $72\%$ in agriculture. Existing models of hours reform tend to abstract from informality \citep{Cacciatore2016} or treat it through a different margin \citep{Ulyssea2018}. This paper applies that concern to a broad cut of the statutory cap.

The model is a static partial-equilibrium accounting framework with structural discipline. It combines a CES aggregator over formal and informal effective labor, an hours-productivity function $e(h)$ peaking at $40$ hours, two firm-size groups, and GHH preferences for welfare. The substitution elasticity is tied to the Brazilian formal-informal wage premium of \citet{MeghirNaritaRobin2015}; the resulting interval should be read as a wage-premium-consistent set rather than as a precisely identified elasticity. The central value is an expositional point, while the headline tables report the joint $(\sigma,\omega,\eta_I)$ envelope.

The central object is $A_{\text{req}}$ as a function of the cap. A move from 44 to 40 hours requires a TFP gain of roughly $2\%$. The 36-hour endpoint is much more demanding: $A_{\text{req}}=6.63\%$ under the one-sided fatigue specification and $8.18\%$ under the two-sided penalty specification. The joint $(\sigma,\omega,\eta_I)$ envelope is $[5.62\%,\,7.48\%]$ under one-sided fatigue and $[6.93\%,\,9.23\%]$ under the two-sided penalty. These numbers are large relative to Brazil's post-1990 TFP record. That comparison is used as a scale benchmark over plausible implementation horizons, not as evidence that any particular reform is infeasible. Aggregate informality rises by $+1.6$ to $1.9$ pp at the 36-hour endpoint, but the main output-cost channel is the mechanical reduction in formal hours rather than reallocation. The formal-informal margin is therefore more important for incidence than for the headline output-neutrality number. The representative agent GHH calculation is positive for moderate cuts and turns negative below $40$h, but it is a calibration diagnostic rather than a social welfare ordering over heterogeneous workers.

Relative to existing hours-reform models, the exercise adds the formal-informal margin and reports the productivity requirement along the hours-cap schedule. It also puts the resulting output-neutrality benchmark on the same scale as Brazil's historical TFP record.

This paper does not estimate the causal effect of PEC 8/2025, PEC 221/2019, or any specific draft that may emerge from the special commission. It also leaves general equilibrium, wage bargaining, worker selection, price adjustment, and capital deepening outside the central model. The narrower goal is to compute the TFP gain that would render alternative hours caps output-neutral under parameters disciplined by Brazilian data. Evidence from Portugal 1996 \citep{AsaiLopesTondini2024} and Brazil 1988 \citep{GonzagaMenezesFilhoCamargo2003} enters as a directional plausibility check rather than as an identified lower bound for Brazil's effective $A_{\text{req}}$.

The main message is therefore conditional. Within this accounting framework, moderate reductions require much less productivity and can generate welfare gains in the representative agent calibration. The 36-hour endpoint requires a productivity gain that is large by recent Brazilian standards.

Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the counterfactual schedule, the TFP benchmark, and the stress test. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} draws conditional implications for reform design.
