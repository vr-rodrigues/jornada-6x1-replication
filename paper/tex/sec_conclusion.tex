\section{Policy implications}\label{sec:conclusion}

The paper quantifies a trade-off the Brazilian policy debate has so far left implicit. Cutting the statutory workweek from 44 to 36 hours affects the majority of Brazilian formal workers and reduces the labor input of the economy by about $14\%$ mechanically. Whether the economy absorbs this cut without an output loss depends on the size of the simultaneous productivity gain. The paper puts a number on that gain. The aggregate output loss is offset by a one-off productivity gain of $6.63\%$ (preferred specification) to $8.18\%$ (conservative upper bound). Neither figure has precedent in the post-1990 Brazilian productivity record. In the best decade after the Real Plan, productivity rose by about a quarter of a percent per year. Matching what the reform requires at that rate would take twenty-five to thirty-one years.

Four messages follow from the model.

First, the $36$-hour proposal lies outside the post-1990 Brazilian productivity record. The reform would require a one-off productivity gain that the country has not produced over three decades. Without that gain, the model predicts a short-run output loss of $6.6$ to $8.0\%$ and an aggregate welfare loss of $1.8$ to $3.6\%$. The productivity requirement is a benchmark, not a forecast of what TFP will do.

Second, a smaller cap is welfare-improving inside the model. Cutting the workweek from $44$ to $41$ hours raises aggregate welfare because the value workers place on extra leisure exceeds the consumption they give up. Below $41$ hours welfare falls. Below $38$ it is negative. The welfare-optimal cap inside the model is near $41$ hours. A cap of $36$ hours overshoots that interior optimum. The interior optimum is internal to the model and does not constitute a policy recommendation.

Third, the cost of the reform is concentrated. Small firms, which employ most formal workers in Brazil, bear the largest per-worker productivity requirement. Industry and agriculture carry the largest per-sector requirements, and informality rises in all three sectors rather than falling. Services, although not the most exposed per worker, account for the bulk of the aggregate output loss through scale. Policy design that ignores this concentration will understate the adjustment burden.

Fourth, the reallocation to informality is material but second-order to the hours channel. The calibrated model, disciplined by the observed formal--informal wage premium, finds an aggregate informality increase of $1.6$--$1.9$ pp. This informalization is material for welfare accounting and small relative to the roughly $15\%$ mechanical hours contraction. The output cost comes primarily from fewer hours worked. The reform also pushes additional workers into informality, weakening its own formalization goals.

These conclusions come with limits. The model is short-run and partial-equilibrium. Endogenous capital adjustment over a three-to-five-year horizon lowers the required productivity gain by about one to two percentage points. Total employment is held fixed, which shuts down work-sharing. The empirical evidence on work-sharing from Germany \citep{Hunt1996} and Brazil's 1988 constitutional reform \citep{GonzagaMenezesFilhoCamargo2003} suggests this restriction is defensible but not innocuous. The Portuguese 1996 experience \citep{AsaiLopesTondini2024} documents firm-level productivity gains larger than the model produces, which suggests the model is conservative on the hourly-productivity response.

Subject to these limits, the model-implied implication is direct. The $44$-to-$36$ reform as currently proposed would require a one-off TFP gain outside the post-1990 Brazilian record. A phased path with an intermediate stop near $41$ hours is more consistent with the model's welfare schedule and would buy time for the capital stock, the informal margin, and firm-level productivity to adjust.
