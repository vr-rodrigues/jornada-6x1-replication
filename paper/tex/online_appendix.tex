\section{Data sources}\label{app:data}

All data are from publicly available sources. PNAD Cont\'{i}nua (IBGE/SIDRA), tables 4093, 4097, 6374, 6389, and 6413, provides the informality rate, position in occupation, average hours, published earnings by formal/informal category pair, firm-size distribution, and the CES formal weight $\omega=0.622$ \citep{PNAD2025}. The package freezes the SIDRA pulls in \texttt{data\_intermediate/pnad\_targets.json} and \texttt{data\_raw/sidra/pnadcm\_6389\_wage\_by\_position\_last.json}. RAIS/CAGED provides administrative records of formal employment, used for firm-size distribution and formal employment shares \citep{RAIS2022}. Since the replication package does not call a public RAIS API, the RAIS-derived shares are documented in \texttt{data\_intermediate/rais\_targets.json}. PWT labor-share evidence, adjusted via \citet{Gollin2002}, gives $\alpha=0.35$. The historical TFP anchor uses PWT 11.0 via the FRED series \texttt{RTFPNABRA632NRUG}, which extends Brazil's \texttt{rtfpna} series to 2023 \citep{PWT2025,FREDPWTBrazil2026}. The FRED input is frozen at \texttt{data\_raw/fred/RTFPNABRA632NRUG\_pwt110.csv}. DIEESE provides contracted-hours categories that the model maps into $\theta=(0.085, 0.269, 0.646)$ for the $\{36,40,44\}$ bins \citep{DIEESE2024}. The mapping assigns the DIEESE $\leq 39$h category to the 36h bin, keeps the 40h category, and assigns the $\geq 41$h category to the 44h bin. An auditing exercise in the replication package verifies that the non-targeted moments in the main text are not algebraic consequences of the calibration.

\section{What partial equilibrium omits}\label{app:pe_omits}

The static partial-equilibrium framework excludes seven general-equilibrium channels. Capital accumulation responds to the lower marginal product of capital after the effective labor contraction. Wages and prices adjust in general equilibrium, partially restoring formal labor demand. Labor force participation may rise with leisure. Monetary and fiscal authorities may accommodate. Workers may reallocate across sectors. The formalization decision may move in either direction. Work-sharing is shut down by the fixed-$N$ assumption. All seven channels except the last have a negative expected sign on $A_{\text{req}}$.

Table~\ref{tab:omitted_channels} separates channels already modeled from those bracketed quantitatively and those discussed qualitatively. The table is deliberately balanced: it includes channels that could lower the output-neutrality benchmark, such as capital adjustment, and channels that could raise or redirect it, such as compliance, overtime scheduling, wage bargaining, and organizational disruption.

\input{tab_omitted_channels_autogen}

The pessimistic TFP stress test lets post-reform TFP fall by $\delta$ before computing the productivity gain required to restore output. It is not a forecast of implementation disruption. It is a robustness exercise for the case in which reorganization, compliance frictions, or learning losses make the transition temporarily less productive.

\input{tab_stress_test_autogen}

The capital-adjustment bound in Equation~\eqref{eq:pe_bound} of the main paper uses a textbook user-cost elasticity $\varepsilon_K\in[0.2,0.4]$ \citep{Caballero1999} and the baseline capital share $\alpha=0.35$. At these elasticities over a $3$ to $5$ year horizon, the effective $A_{\text{req}}$ under the two-sided penalty specification falls from $8.18\%$ to between $6.4\%$ and $7.2\%$, and under the one-sided fatigue specification to between $4.8\%$ and $5.6\%$. The remaining six channels are individually small and of mixed sign. The headline numbers should be read as short-run upper bounds on the required TFP gain. The $1$ to $2$ year model horizon is not the same as the historical PWT window used to anchor $A_{\text{req}}$. The anchor is best read as the cumulative TFP gain required over a $3$ to $10$ year phase-in horizon.

\section{Calibration details}\label{app:calibration_details}

\subsection{Hours-output elasticity and efficiency shape}

The Pencavel evidence documents declining marginal product of hours at long workweeks for a specific industrial setting. \citet{CollewetSauermann2017} find analogous long-hour productivity losses in a Dutch call-center panel. \citet{BickFuchsSchundelnLagakos2018} provide cross-country evidence consistent with declining marginal product of hours, though their primary focus is the relationship between hours and income. Neither paper identifies a precise peak location or an efficiency response below a peak. The choice to place the peak at $h^*=40$ and to compare a one-sided fatigue specification (no penalty for shorter hours) with a two-sided penalty specification (equal curvature either side) is a modeling choice loosely informed by this evidence rather than a direct estimate from it. Section~\ref{app:hours_efficiency} reports four alternative shapes.

\subsection{Informal efficiency and adjustment costs}

The informal efficiency $\eta_I=0.40$ is set at the midpoint of the informal-to-formal wage ratio range ($1/3$ to $1/2$) reported by \citet{LaPortaShleifer2014}, complemented by the Mexican estimates in \citet{BussoFazioLevy2012}. Under competitive wage-taking the CES first-order conditions identify $\eta_I$ through the wage (marginal-product) ratio rather than through value-added per employee, which conflates productivity differences with capital-per-worker gaps between formal and informal firms. The adjustment costs $\gamma_F^S=0.12$ and $\gamma_F^L=0.03$ reflect the effective marginal compliance cost of CLT/FGTS obligations for small and large firms. Sensitivity over $\gamma_F^S\in[0.08,0.16]$ and $\gamma_F^L\in[0.01,0.05]$ shifts $A_{\text{req}}$ by less than $\pm 0.2$ pp. The convex informality cost $\pi_m$ is calibrated endogenously to ensure non-negative wedges at the informality target.

\subsection{Reconciliation with higher substitution elasticities}\label{app:sigma_discussion}

Refugee-crisis studies report substitution elasticities between worker types of $\sigma\approx 11$ to $30$ \citep{OttavianoPeri2012, BaharDiTellaGulek2025}. These magnitudes are not directly comparable to $\sigma_{\text{sub}}$ as defined here. Refugee studies measure substitution between workers of different origin in a market-clearing equilibrium. Here, $\sigma_{\text{sub}}$ captures intra-firm substitution between formal and informal employment regimes. The horizon also differs, since refugee studies identify medium-run responses that include firm entry and organizational reallocation, while the model here holds the firm distribution fixed. The unit differs as well, since refugee studies typically include self-employed workers, while the aggregator concerns salaried employment. DSGE models of informality \citep{LeyvaUrrutia2020, McKiernan2021} similarly use $\sigma$ parameters that govern sectoral mobility rather than intra-firm substitution. In \citet{Ulyssea2018}, formal and informal workers are effectively perfect substitutes within the firm, with productivity differences captured entirely by the distribution of firm types. The calibration $\sigma=1.326$ with $\eta_I=0.40$ can be read as a parametric decomposition in which the formal-informal wage ratio pins the per-efficiency-unit relative productivity of informal labor, while modest intra-firm substitutability ($\sigma>1$) absorbs the remaining reallocation margin. Both are disciplined by the observed wage premium.

\subsection{Disciplining $\sigma_{\text{sub}}$: full results}

For each $\sigma$ in a grid over $[0.40,3.00]$, the full model is recalibrated and the implied formal-informal wage premium is computed from the CES first-order condition,
\[
R(\sigma)=\frac{\omega}{1-\omega}\cdot\left(\frac{L_F}{L_I}\right)^{\rho-1}\cdot z,
\]
where $z=\text{eff}_{hF}/(\eta_I h_I e(h_I))$. The mapping is monotone in $\sigma$. At the recalibrated $(\omega,\eta_I)=(0.622,0.40)$, setting $R\in[1.15,1.55]$ delivers $\sigma\in[1.116,1.469]$ and the conservative midbox $A_{\text{req}}\in[7.50\%,8.58\%]$ (the full $(\sigma,\omega,\eta_I)$-box envelope is $[6.93\%,9.23\%]$). \citet{DerenoncourtGerardLagosMontialoux2025} estimate a small formal-to-informal reallocation elasticity of about $-0.28$ from the Brazilian minimum-wage passthrough, consistent with the modest intra-firm $\sigma$ used in this paper.

The wage-premium bridge compares current published PNAD earnings with the MNR window. These ratios are not used as controlled estimates of the formal premium. They check whether the private-employee and domestic-worker category pairs place current Brazilian formal/informal earnings gaps close to the structural window used to discipline $\sigma_{\text{sub}}$. The self-employed and employer pairs are shown for context but not used for identification because their earnings mix labor income with capital, scale, and client composition.

\section{Welfare: type-level view}\label{app:welfare_additional}

The type-level decomposition uses worker shares and wage-bill shares under the conservative $44\to 36$h reform. Workers who remain formal work approximately six fewer hours per week and gain in the first-order incidence calculation. Informal workers' welfare is largely unchanged because $h_I=44$ is not subject to the cap. The mass of workers deformalized at the margin faces sharp losses. At the moderate $\eta_I=0.40$ this mass is small but quantitatively important for incidence. The type-level figures are a first-order approximation. The exact representative-agent CV in Equation~\eqref{eq:cv} of the main paper computes the composite at aggregate consumption and hours, so it is not the weighted sum of the type-level rows.

\section{Derivations}\label{app:derivations}

\subsection{$\kappa$ from $e_q$}\label{app:kappa_deriv}

With $e(h)=\exp\{-\kappa(h-h^*)^2\}$, the hours--output elasticity at $h_{\text{ref}}$ is
\[
e_q = \frac{d\ln(h\cdot e(h))}{d\ln h}\bigg|_{h=h_{\text{ref}}} = 1 - 2\kappa\,h_{\text{ref}}(h_{\text{ref}}-h^*),
\]
so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa_derivation}
\kappa=\frac{1-e_q}{2 h_{\text{ref}}(h_{\text{ref}}-h^*)}.
\end{equation}
With $e_q=0.60$, $h_{\text{ref}}=42.244$, and $h^*=40$, $\kappa=2.110\times 10^{-3}$, so $e(36)=e(44)=0.9668$ and $e(40)=1$.

\subsection{Firm first-order condition}

The firm problem has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:foc_NF}
\frac{\partial Y_g}{\partial L_{F,g}}\cdot h_{F,\text{avg}}\cdot e_F\cdot\omega\cdot\left(\frac{L_{F,g}}{L_g}\right)^{\rho-1}=\tau_g+\gamma_{F,g}(N_{F,g}-\bar{N}_{F,g})+\pi_m N_{I,g},
\end{equation}
where $\partial Y_g/\partial L_{F,g}=(1-\alpha)Y_g/L_g\cdot(\partial L_g/\partial L_{F,g})$ and $\partial L_g/\partial L_{F,g}=\omega(L_{F,g}/L_g)^{\rho-1}$.

\subsection{$A_{\text{req}}$ bisection monotonicity}

$A_{\text{req}}$ solves Equation~\eqref{eq:areq} of the main paper. The Cobb-Douglas structure $Y_g=A K_g^\alpha L_g^{1-\alpha}$ is linear in $A$ and the optimal $L_g(A)$ is weakly increasing in $A$, so $\partial\sum_g Y_g/\partial A>0$ and bisection converges to a unique $A_{\text{req}}$.
